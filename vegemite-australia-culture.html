VEGEMITE

A thin scrape of black paste, and suddenly you know whether you're a tourist or you're not.

1923Year it first went on shelves
1Rule -- thin scrape, not a thick spread
100%Australian, always has been
THE PASTE THAT SORTS TOURISTS FROM LOCALS

Every visitor to Australia gets handed a knife, a piece of toast, and a jar of something the color of tar. Vegemite doesn't explain itself. It doesn't try to. Salty, savory, faintly bitter -- a flavor built entirely out of spent brewer's yeast, with no interest in being liked by people who weren't raised on it.

A CHEMIST'S ACCIDENT, A NATION'S OBSESSION

In 1922, a food company hired a young chemist named Cyril Callister to solve a supply problem -- British Marmite had stopped arriving after the war, and Australia needed its own yeast spread. He built one out of leftover brewer's yeast, celery, onion, and salt. It hit shelves in 1923. A hundred years later, it's less a condiment than a citizenship test.

"Love it or hate it, it's iconic" isn't marketing copy. It's just the honest split every Australian household has argued about since 1923.
HOW TO ACTUALLY EAT IT

The number one mistake tourists make: spreading it like peanut butter. Don't. Butter first, then a scrape of Vegemite so thin you can see the toast through it. A little goes a long way -- deceptively far, actually, since most first-timers pile on three times what any Australian would.

BLUE MOSQUE

Sultan Ahmed I wanted minarets made of gold. What he got instead was six of them -- and a diplomatic headache with Mecca that took a seventh minaret to fix.

A SULTAN WITH SOMETHING TO PROVE

Ahmed I took the Ottoman throne at fourteen and by his early twenties had a problem: no major military victory to his name, at a moment when sultans were expected to fund grand mosques with the spoils of conquest. He built one anyway, paying for it out of the state treasury instead -- unusual enough that it drew criticism from religious scholars at the time. Construction ran from 1609 to 1616, on the site of the old Byzantine Great Palace, directly across the square from the Hagia Sophia it was clearly built to rival.

GOLD, MISHEARD AS SIX

The mosque's architect was Sedefkar Mehmed Agha, a former student of the legendary Ottoman master builder Mimar Sinan. Local legend says Ahmed asked for minarets in altın (gold) and Mehmed Agha heard altı (six) -- a one-letter mix-up that produced one of the most photographed skylines in the world. True or not, the result was a real problem: six minarets matched the count at the Masjid al-Haram in Mecca, Islam's holiest site, and no other mosque was supposed to equal it. Ahmed's fix was to fund a seventh minaret for Mecca, restoring its rank and letting Istanbul keep its six.

The story may be a myth. The six minarets are not. Istanbul's skyline has carried the mix-up for over four hundred years.
WHY IT'S ACTUALLY BLUE

The nickname comes from the interior, not the exterior. More than 20,000 handmade Iznik tiles line the upper walls and dome in over fifty tulip and floral patterns, cut in the blue-dominant palette Iznik workshops were famous for. Demand for the tiles was so high during construction that prices were fixed by imperial decree -- and, according to some accounts, quality slipped in the later batches once workshops were rushing to keep up. Light pours in through 260 windows, originally fitted with hand-blown Venetian glass, giving the whole space its signature dim, blue-tinted glow.

STILL A WORKING MOSQUE

Locally it's the Sultan Ahmed Mosque, or Sultanahmet Camii -- "Blue Mosque" is a Western nickname that never caught on in Turkish. It has never stopped being a functioning mosque: the building closes to sightseers roughly an hour around each of the five daily prayers, longer on Friday for the midday congregational prayer. A 2015–2023 restoration cleaned centuries of soot off the tiles and dome and reopened the outer courtyard, so the interior now reads closer to how it looked in 1616 than it has in generations. Entry is free, no ticket or museum pass required, though there's no skipping the line. Women are asked to cover their hair (scarves are provided free at the entrance) and everyone is asked to dress modestly -- shoulders and knees covered, shoes off at the door. Along with the Hagia Sophia across the square, it's part of the Historic Areas of Istanbul, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1985, and still draws millions of visitors a year who file in quietly between prayers to look up at the same tiled dome Ahmed I never got his gold minarets to match.
